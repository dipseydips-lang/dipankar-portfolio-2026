How to Choose an SEO Company Without Getting Burned: A Buyer's Guide From the Inside

by Dipankar Mukherji·SEO strategy·~6 min read·target keyword: how to choose an SEO company

I write SEO content for a living, which means I have spent years on the inside of the industry you are about to buy from. I have seen the work that earns a client's renewal for the fifth straight year, and I have seen the retainers that were, politely, subscription-priced disappointment. This guide on how to choose an SEO company is everything I would tell a friend before they signed anything.

Start with an uncomfortable fact

Many businesses paying for SEO are not getting SEO. In technical audits of websites that had been "doing SEO" with an agency for over a year, I routinely find 12 to 18 unresolved indexation and on-page issues: pages Google cannot see, duplicate titles, broken internal links, orphaned content. Basics, unfixed, while monthly reports kept arriving on time.

The lesson is not "agencies are bad." Great ones exist and they are worth every rupee or dollar. The lesson is that reports and results are different products, and you need to know how to tell which one you are buying.

The five questions that expose everything

1. "Walk me through what you did for a client in month three."

Not month one, when everyone does an audit. Not the case study highlight reel. Month three, when the honeymoon is over. A real agency describes specific pages built, fixes shipped and content published. A report factory describes "ongoing optimization activities."

2. "Which number will you report that you cannot make look good if the work is failing?"

Impressions always go up. Traffic can rise while leads flatline. The only honest anchors are qualified leads, pipeline and revenue from organic. Anyone worth hiring answers this in one sentence. Anyone who pivots to "visibility" just answered a different question, and also this one.

3. "Who exactly will work on my account, and how many accounts do they handle?"

The people in the sales call are rarely the people doing the work. Ask to meet the actual strategist and writer. An account handler juggling 25 clients can give yours about 90 minutes a week. That is not a strategy. That is a rotation.

4. "Show me content you wrote for a niche as boring as mine."

Anyone can write about travel. Ask for their industrial valve content, their façade engineering blog, their elevator interiors page. If they can make an unglamorous niche read well and rank, they can do it for you. If every sample is a lifestyle blog, keep looking.

5. "What will you need from us?"

Trick question. The wrong answer is "nothing, we handle everything." Great SEO needs your subject-matter expertise, your customer questions, your data. An agency that plans to work without your input is planning to write generic content, and generic content is a commodity that ranks like one.

Red flags that predict a wasted year

  • Guaranteed rankings or "page one in 30 days." Google itself says no one can guarantee rankings. This pitch selects for either dishonesty or tactics you will pay for later.
  • Secret methods. "Proprietary techniques we cannot reveal" translates to techniques that would not survive being revealed.
  • Cheap monthly packages with big deliverable counts. Fifteen blogs a month for the price of one good one means AI output with a human glance. It fills a sitemap, not a pipeline.
  • Reports without recommendations. A report that only says what happened, never what to do next, is a screenshot service.
  • No questions about your business. If the discovery call is all pitch and no curiosity about your margins, your buyers and your capacity, they are selling a package, not building your strategy.

A simple scorecard for comparing finalists

Price10%Last, on purpose. Cheap SEO is expensive.

Agency, freelancer or consultant?

Agencies bring teams and processes, at the cost of divided attention. Freelancers bring focus and accountability, at the cost of bandwidth. A senior consultant who strategizes, writes and builds the workflow sits in between, and for many small and mid-sized businesses it is the highest leverage per rupee spent. Naturally I would say that. But now you have five questions and a scorecard, so you do not have to take my word for it. Ask me the hard questions too.

Understanding pricing models before you compare quotes

Quotes confuse buyers because agencies price in different shapes. Decode them like this:

  • Monthly retainer ($1,500 to $7,500+ for small and mid-sized businesses): the standard. Sensible for SEO's ongoing nature. Insist on an itemized scope: hours or deliverables, named owners, and a quarterly strategy review baked in.
  • Project pricing (audits, migrations, one-time content builds): ideal for defined jobs with clear endpoints. A properly scoped technical audit at $500 to $2,000 is often the smartest first purchase, because its output makes every retainer conversation concrete.
  • Hourly consulting ($75 to $250 per hour): best for businesses with in-house hands who need senior direction. You buy judgment, your team executes, and none of the budget evaporates into agency overhead.
  • Performance pricing ("pay per ranking" or "pay per lead"): treat with suspicion. It incentivizes chasing easy, low-value keywords and counting junk leads. The version of this that works is a hybrid: modest base plus bonuses tied to metrics you defined, qualified leads, not positions.

The first 90 days: what you should actually see

Once you sign, judge the engagement against this timeline rather than against vibes:

Days 1 to 30: a real technical audit with a prioritized fix list, keyword and competitor research you can read and challenge, access sorted for analytics and Search Console, and a content plan mapped to actual queries with actual intent labels. If month one produces only a kickoff call and a template report, you have already learned what the next eleven months will look like.

Days 31 to 60: priority technical fixes shipped and verified, first content published or existing money pages upgraded, and a baseline report that honestly documents where everything started so improvement is measurable rather than asserted.

Days 61 to 90: early movement on long-tail rankings, indexation of new content confirmed, and, critically, a strategy conversation that adapts based on what the first two months revealed. A plan that never changes is a plan nobody is looking at.

What you should not expect in 90 days: dramatic lead growth in a competitive niche. What you should absolutely expect: visible work, honest numbers and increasing clarity. Fire for absence of the second list, not the first.

Questions agencies hope you never ask

  • "Can I speak to a client you have had for over two years?" Longevity is the review that cannot be faked.
  • "Can I see a report you sent a client in a bad month?" How they communicate failure tells you everything about the partnership ahead.
  • "Who owns the content, accounts and links if we part ways?" The only acceptable answer is you. Get it in the contract.
  • "What would make you tell us to pause or reduce our retainer?" Honest vendors have an answer. Salespeople find the question confusing.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a small business pay for SEO?

Credible retainers for small businesses generally start around $1,500 per month in the US market. Below roughly $1,000, the economics cannot cover competent labor, and the corner being cut is usually the content and the fixes, meaning the entire point.

Is it better to hire a local SEO company?

Local knowledge helps for local campaigns, but competence beats geography. A skilled remote team with a documented process will outperform a mediocre agency across town every time. Judge the work, not the office address.

How do I check an agency's own SEO?

Search their money keywords and see if they practice what they sell. Then apply nuance: great agencies sometimes rank modestly because client work eats their capacity. Treat their own rankings as one signal, their client evidence as the verdict.

When should I fire my SEO company?

After 90 days of activity without visible work product, after any discovered guideline-violating tactic done without your consent, or after two consecutive quarterly reviews where the strategy did not change despite the numbers saying it should. Reports arriving on schedule is not a reason to stay. It is the minimum.
