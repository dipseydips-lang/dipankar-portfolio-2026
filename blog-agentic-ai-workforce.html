AI, Software & Work

Agentic AI Is Coming for Software First. The Workforce Question Comes Next.

The most interesting effect of AI agents may not be that they replace a particular job. It may be that they change what a company considers a software task in the first place.

Software has spent decades turning human work into interfaces.

A person needs to book a flight, so the software provides a search box, filters, calendars and payment screens. A sales representative needs to update a customer record, so the CRM provides forms and workflows. A developer needs to investigate a bug, so the development environment provides logs, terminals, repositories and issue trackers.

The software is the middle layer between the person and the task.

Agentic AI starts to challenge that arrangement.

Instead of learning how to operate five different systems, a person can describe the outcome they want and let an agent coordinate the systems required to produce it. That sounds like another productivity improvement, but it has a deeper consequence. If the interface becomes conversational and the execution layer becomes increasingly autonomous, the value of some software products may move away from screens and toward access, context, integrations, permissions and outcomes.

That is where the workforce question becomes more interesting.

The question is not simply, “Will AI take software jobs?” A better question is: What happens to the layers of work that existed because humans had to operate software manually?

The software industry has already started moving in this direction

This is no longer a theoretical discussion about a distant version of AI.

Gartner reported in 2025 that AI-native software engineering was beginning to reshape the software development lifecycle, with AI being embedded across activities from design through deployment. Its forecast said that by 2028, 90% of enterprise software engineers would use AI code assistants, compared with less than 14% in early 2024. Gartner also described a shift in the developer role from implementation toward orchestration, problem solving and system design.

That prediction matters because software development is only one part of a much larger pattern.

OpenAI's agent tooling has moved beyond simple text generation toward systems that can use web search, file search, computer interaction and other tools as part of multi-step tasks. Anthropic has similarly described agents as systems that can plan, use tools, receive feedback from the environment and continue working toward a goal.

These developments are important for a reason that is easy to miss: the unit of automation is changing.

Traditional automation usually starts with a process.

If X happens, do Y.

Generative AI introduced a different model.

Give the system information, and it can produce an answer.

Agents add another layer.

Give the system a goal, and it can decide which actions are needed to get there.

That last shift is the one that has consequences for software, organizations and jobs.

Think about what happens when the task becomes the interface

Consider a simple example from finance.

A finance employee currently works across accounting software, spreadsheets, email, banking portals and reporting tools. The employee may spend part of the day moving information from one system to another, checking discrepancies, preparing reports and asking colleagues for missing details.

Now imagine an agent with permission to access those systems.

The instruction is not:

Open the accounting platform. Export the transactions. Compare them with the bank statement. Flag mismatches. Email the relevant department. Prepare a weekly report.

The instruction becomes:

Reconcile this week's transactions, identify unusual discrepancies, prepare the report and send the exceptions to the appropriate owners for review.

The difference is subtle on the surface but enormous underneath.

The user is no longer interacting with each application as the primary interface. The agent is.

That does not make the accounting platform irrelevant. The underlying data, permissions, audit trails, controls and business logic still matter. But the visible workflow can become much less dependent on a human knowing where each action belongs.

This pattern can appear in software development, customer support, procurement, sales operations, IT administration, research and many other functions.

It is also why the first impact of agents may be felt inside software workflows before it becomes obvious in employment statistics.

The first jobs affected may be bundles of tasks, not job titles

Job titles are convenient for organizations. They are less useful for understanding automation.

A content operations specialist, for example, might spend time researching topics, collecting source material, creating briefs, entering information into a CMS, checking metadata, updating internal documents and reporting performance.

Those tasks do not have the same automation profile.

Some are highly structured. Some require judgement. Some depend on organizational context. Some carry reputational risk. Some require collaboration with another human.

An agent can potentially handle parts of the structured workflow while leaving the high-context decisions to people.

This is why the phrase “AI will replace jobs” can be misleading when used without looking at the actual work inside those jobs.

A better model is task compression.

Ten manual steps may become three supervised steps. A two-hour workflow may become a fifteen-minute review. A process that previously required three systems specialists may require one person who knows how to define the objective, configure the permissions and validate the result.

The headcount effect depends on what the organization does with that released capacity.

That last sentence is where strategy enters the picture.

There is a second possibility: companies may build what they used to buy

Software has traditionally been sold as a package because building everything internally is expensive.

A company buys a CRM instead of creating one. It buys an analytics platform instead of maintaining its own data tooling. It subscribes to workflow software instead of building an internal application for every operational process.

Agents do not automatically reverse that model, but they can lower the cost of building narrowly useful internal tools.

Suppose a company needs a small internal system that reads support tickets, classifies them, checks a knowledge base, drafts a response and routes complex cases to a specialist.

Historically, the organization might have evaluated a customer support platform or commissioned a custom application.

With modern models, APIs, retrieval systems and agent frameworks, the organization can potentially assemble a smaller purpose-built system around its own data and workflow.

That creates an uncomfortable question for software vendors:

What happens when the cost of building a 20% version of a software product becomes dramatically lower?

The answer is not that SaaS disappears. Specialized software still has major advantages in reliability, security, integrations, compliance, support and scale.

But the boundary between “buy” and “build” could become less rigid.

Anthropic's research on effective agents makes a related point from an engineering perspective: the most effective systems are not necessarily the most complicated ones. Simple, composable patterns can be more useful than adding layers of complexity simply because an agent framework makes them available.

That idea has a commercial implication. If organizations can assemble focused systems more cheaply, software vendors may increasingly compete on infrastructure, trust, data, ecosystem access and depth of capability rather than merely on the number of features visible in a dashboard.

Developers are not disappearing. The definition of development is expanding.

There is a strange contradiction in discussions about AI coding agents.

On one side, agents are becoming capable of handling increasingly large pieces of software work. On the other, the better these systems become, the more important architecture, testing, security and review become.

Writing code is only one part of building software.

Someone still has to decide what should be built. Someone has to understand the constraints. Someone has to determine whether the result is safe. Someone has to decide whether the generated solution fits the existing architecture. Someone has to deal with the consequences when an apparently correct change breaks a business process.

Gartner's 2025 software engineering research points toward this shift from implementation toward orchestration and system-level responsibility.

This changes what senior technical talent may look like.

The valuable developer of the next few years may spend less time typing routine code and more time doing things such as:

  • Defining system boundaries and requirements
  • Designing agent and tool interfaces
  • Creating evaluation datasets
  • Reviewing generated changes
  • Managing permissions and security controls
  • Designing recovery paths when automation fails
  • Understanding business processes deeply enough to judge outcomes

That is not the disappearance of engineering. It is engineering moving upward in the stack.

The real workforce problem may be the gap between automation and judgment

There is a category of work that becomes more valuable when automation improves: judgment.

Not because humans are magically better at everything, but because automated systems create a need for someone to decide when the system should be trusted.

Imagine an agent that resolves 92% of support tickets correctly.

That sounds impressive.

But the remaining 8% may contain the most expensive cases: account disputes, security incidents, legal complaints, high-value customers or unusual technical failures.

The organization therefore needs a different operating model. Humans may handle fewer routine cases while becoming more concentrated around exceptions.

This changes training requirements.

A person who only knows how to execute the standard workflow may become less valuable as the standard workflow gets automated. A person who understands why the workflow exists, where it can fail and what to do when it fails becomes more valuable.

This is one reason domain knowledge matters so much in an agentic organization.

The uncomfortable part: not every organization will use the productivity gain well

Technology does not decide whether productivity becomes better jobs, fewer jobs, higher margins, faster growth or simply more work.

Management decisions do.

A company that uses agents to remove repetitive work can give employees more time for product development, customer relationships, research and problem solving.

Another company can use exactly the same technology to increase workloads, reduce headcount and expect the remaining employees to supervise a growing collection of automated systems.

Both outcomes are technically possible.

This is why the workforce conversation should not stop at model capability.

The important questions are organizational:

  • Which tasks are being automated?
  • Who owns the outcome?
  • Who reviews failures?
  • What new skills are employees expected to develop?
  • What happens to the time that automation creates?
  • Which decisions remain human by design?

An organization that cannot answer these questions has an automation strategy, but not necessarily a workforce strategy.

What becomes valuable when software can act?

If agents become a normal part of software, several skills should become more valuable rather than less.

1. Problem definition

Agents can execute a poorly defined task very efficiently and still produce the wrong outcome. The ability to translate a business problem into a clear objective, constraints, inputs and success criteria becomes increasingly important.

2. Systems thinking

An agent rarely operates in isolation. It touches databases, APIs, applications, documents, people and policies. Understanding how those pieces interact becomes more important than knowing how to operate one interface perfectly.

3. Evaluation

When software can act autonomously, testing cannot be limited to whether a screen loads or an API returns a successful response. Teams need to evaluate outcomes, tool use, safety, failure recovery and consistency.

4. Domain expertise

The more capable the automation becomes, the more useful it is to have people who understand the domain well enough to recognize subtle errors. A generic AI skill set is not always enough.

5. Communication

Agentic systems cross organizational boundaries. Technical teams have to explain capabilities and risks to business teams. Business teams have to express goals and constraints clearly enough for technical teams to encode them.

There is also a new kind of software literacy emerging

For years, digital literacy largely meant knowing how to use software.

The emerging skill may be knowing how to work with software that can act.

That means understanding permissions, context, tool access, verification, escalation and delegation.

It also means knowing when not to delegate.

A useful agentic workflow is not one where the agent does everything. It is one where responsibility is divided deliberately.

The agent might gather information, compare records, perform routine transformations and prepare a recommendation. A human might approve a financial transaction, interpret an ambiguous customer situation or make a decision with regulatory consequences.

That boundary will differ by organization and task.

But it should be designed rather than discovered accidentally after something goes wrong.

The next software battle may be about outcomes, not interfaces

This may be the biggest commercial consequence of the shift.

For years, software products have competed through interfaces and feature sets. Better dashboards. More automation rules. More integrations. More reports. More configuration options.

Agents introduce another possibility.

A company could increasingly ask software to deliver an outcome.

Instead of buying another dashboard, it might want an agent that continuously identifies revenue anomalies and sends only the cases that need attention.

Instead of buying another project-management feature, it might want an agent that monitors project activity, identifies blocked work and coordinates follow-ups.

Instead of buying another reporting workflow, it might want an agent that gathers data from several systems and produces a decision-ready briefing.

The underlying software remains important. But the customer's mental model changes from using a tool to delegating a task.

That is a meaningful change in how software could be designed, priced and evaluated.

What should companies do now?

The sensible response is not to give every employee an agent and wait for the organization to transform itself.

A better starting point is to map work.

Take one important workflow and break it into individual tasks. Identify which tasks are repetitive, which require judgement, which depend on external systems and which create unacceptable risk if automated incorrectly.

Then automate the smallest useful part.

Measure the result.

Expand only when the evidence supports expansion.

This approach also prevents a common mistake in agentic AI adoption: choosing the technology before defining the problem.

The goal should not be “we need an agent.” The goal should be something measurable such as reducing manual reconciliation time, shortening software testing cycles, improving response times or reducing administrative work.

The agent is a means to that end.

The workforce question is therefore not a yes-or-no question

Agentic AI will probably remove some tasks. It will compress others. It will create new responsibilities around evaluation, supervision, architecture and governance. It may also make some software products easier to replace with focused internal systems.

None of this produces a simple prediction about employment.

What it does suggest is that the relationship between people and software is changing.

For a long time, people adapted themselves to software. They learned the menus, workflows, forms and rules of the systems their organizations purchased.

Agents begin to reverse that relationship.

Software is increasingly expected to understand the user's objective, navigate systems and perform work on the user's behalf.

That is why the workforce discussion matters.

The biggest change may not be that machines suddenly become capable of doing every job. It may be that organizations stop designing jobs around the limitations of software interfaces.

Once that happens, the question becomes much more interesting:

If software can take on more of the execution, what should humans spend their time being responsible for?

That is a question companies will have to answer long before agentic AI reaches its theoretical limits.
