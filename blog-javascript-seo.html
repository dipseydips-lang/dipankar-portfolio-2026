JavaScript SEO: What Google Actually Sees When Your Beautiful React Site Loads a Blank Page

by Dipankar Mukherji·technical SEO·~13 min read·target keyword: JavaScript SEO

There is a specific kind of silence that falls over a meeting when you show a development team what their site looks like with JavaScript disabled. The designers see a blank white rectangle. The developers see a perfectly normal loading state. And whoever owns the traffic numbers slowly realizes why six months of content investment has produced almost nothing.

I am a content and SEO person, not a framework engineer, and I want to be upfront about that, because it is exactly why this guide exists. The best JavaScript SEO explanations are written by developers for developers, and they sail straight over the heads of the marketers who first notice the symptoms. This is the version for the rest of us: enough technical depth to diagnose real problems and hold a real conversation with an engineering team, without pretending you are about to rewrite the build pipeline yourself.

The core problem in one paragraph

Traditional websites send finished HTML to whoever asks: browser, crawler, anyone. Modern JavaScript frameworks often send something closer to an empty stage plus a set of instructions, and the visitor's browser builds the page on arrival. Humans with modern browsers never notice. Crawlers, scrapers and AI systems asking for your page get the empty stage, and whether they stick around to execute the instructions varies from "eventually" to "never." Every JavaScript SEO problem I have ever diagnosed is a variation of that gap between what was sent and what was meant.

How Google actually processes JavaScript (the two-wave reality)

Google can render JavaScript, and has been able to for years. The nuance that matters is when and at what cost.

Crawling happens in effectively two waves. In the first, Googlebot fetches your raw HTML and indexes whatever is there: text, links, metadata. Pages then queue for a second wave, where a headless Chromium renders the JavaScript and Google indexes what appears afterward. That rendering queue is real, it is shared with the entire internet, and your position in it is a function of your site's perceived importance. For a major publisher, the gap between waves might be trivial. For an ordinary business site, rendered content can lag raw content by hours or days, and on large sites, some pages may simply never get the full treatment on a useful schedule.

Three practical consequences fall out of this:

  • Anything critical that exists only after rendering is a second-class citizen. Title tags, meta descriptions, canonical tags, primary content and internal links injected purely client-side get discovered late, inconsistently, or not at all.
  • Internal link discovery suffers most. If your navigation and content links only exist post-render, Google's map of your site is built on delayed information. I have audited sites where entire sections were near-invisible for exactly this reason, and everyone had blamed the content.
  • Rendering costs crawl budget. On sites with tens of thousands of URLs, forcing Google to render everything is like making every guest assemble their own chair before sitting down. Some will. Many will find another party.

And here is the 2026 twist that changes the stakes: Google is no longer the only machine reading your site. AI assistants, answer engines and the crawlers feeding them are often far less patient with JavaScript than Googlebot is. Several prominent AI crawlers execute little or no JavaScript at all. A site that is technically fine for Google can be a complete void to the systems that increasingly answer your buyers' questions. If AI visibility is on your roadmap, and it should be, server-delivered content stopped being optional.

The rendering menu, translated from engineering

When you raise this with a development team, the conversation will involve four acronyms. Here is what each actually means for the content and SEO outcome, which is the only translation most stakeholders need.

Client-side rendering (CSR): the browser builds everything. Fastest to develop, riskiest for search. Acceptable for logged-in dashboards and app screens that should never rank anyway. For public content pages, it is the configuration that produces the blank-rectangle meeting.

Server-side rendering (SSR): the server builds the full HTML per request, JavaScript then takes over for interactivity. Crawlers and AI systems get real content immediately. The cost is server load and engineering complexity, which is why developers sigh when you ask for it. Modern frameworks have made it dramatically easier than it was five years ago, which is worth saying in the meeting.

Static site generation (SSG): pages get pre-built into plain HTML at deploy time. For content that changes on publishing schedules rather than by the second, blogs, marketing pages, documentation, this is the sweet spot: crawler-perfect, blazing fast, cheap to serve. Its limitation is content that changes constantly or is personalized per visitor.

Incremental and hybrid approaches: the practical modern answer for most real businesses. Marketing and content routes get static or server rendering; the app behind the login stays client-side. Product pages regenerate on a schedule or on demand. Nearly every framework supports this route-by-route decision now, which means the old all-or-nothing argument is obsolete, and you should politely refuse to have it.

The rule of thumb I bring to these discussions: if a URL is supposed to earn traffic, its meaningful content should be in the HTML the server sends. Everything else is negotiable. That single sentence has settled more architecture debates than any slide deck I have made.

Hydration, and the uncanny valley of "it looks fine"

Server rendering introduces its own trap, and it is sneaky because everything looks correct. Hydration is the process where the browser's JavaScript takes over the server-sent HTML and makes it interactive. When hydration goes wrong, you get pages that render, then flicker, shift, or silently swap their content, because the client-side application disagreed with what the server sent.

Why should a content person care about a developer bug? Two reasons. First, hydration mismatches can mean the crawler-visible version and the user-visible version of a page differ, which at best confuses indexing and at worst looks like cloaking. Second, heavy hydration is a notorious performance tax: the page appears quickly but refuses to respond to taps for seconds, which shows up directly in interaction metrics that feed rankings. If your site scores well on paint metrics but badly on responsiveness, hydration cost is one of the first suspects to interrogate.

The diagnostic routine (no engineering degree required)

Here is the exact sequence I run before ever raising JavaScript concerns with a client or a dev team, because arriving with evidence changes the entire conversation.

1. The view-source test. Open a money page, view the raw page source (not the browser inspector, the actual source), and search for a sentence from the main content. Present? Good, the server is sending it. Absent while the inspector shows it? The content is client-rendered, and you have found the gap in thirty seconds.

2. The URL inspection test. Google Search Console's URL inspection shows the rendered HTML and a screenshot as Google sees it. Compare against the live page. Missing sections, absent links and default meta tags show up here in embarrassing clarity.

3. The JavaScript-off walkthrough. Disable JavaScript in the browser and try to navigate the site. Can you reach every important page through real links? Does primary content exist? This approximates the least patient machines reading your site, which in 2026 includes several AI crawlers you care about.

4. The crawler comparison. Run a crawl of the site twice with any mainstream SEO crawler: once plain, once with rendering enabled. Diff the discovered URLs, titles and word counts. The delta between those crawls is, precisely, your JavaScript dependency, itemized into a spreadsheet a CTO cannot argue with.

5. The log check, if you can get it. Server logs reveal what crawlers actually fetch and how often. Rendered-only sections show their neglect here first. On one audit, log data showed an entire client-rendered category tree receiving a small fraction of the crawl attention its raw-HTML siblings got, which explained a traffic mystery that content changes had failed to solve for a year.

The specific failures I keep finding in the wild

Patterns from real audits, in rough order of frequency:

  • Meta tags managed client-side. The framework sets titles and descriptions after load, so the raw HTML carries one default title sitewide. Search results show it. Nobody had looked.
  • Links that are not links. Navigation built on click handlers and buttons instead of real anchor tags with href attributes. Users navigate happily; crawlers see a dead end. If it does not have an href, to a machine it is furniture.
  • Infinite scroll with no paginated fallback. Products load as humans scroll, and no crawlable path exists to anything beyond the first screen. Entire catalogs go dark past item twenty.
  • Content behind interaction. Tabs, accordions and load-more buttons whose content is fetched only on click. Crawlers do not click. Whatever lives behind that interaction does not exist.
  • Redirects and canonicals executed in JavaScript. Signals that should live in HTTP headers or raw HTML being applied post-render, which means the first indexing wave never sees them at all.
  • The staging robots block, promoted to production. Not strictly a JavaScript issue, but it travels with the same deploy pipelines, and it has produced more sudden traffic funerals than any framework choice ever has.

How to have the conversation with developers (without the eye-rolling)

The predictable failure mode of this topic is a content person marching into engineering with "Google says" and a listicle. Having sat through the productive version and the disastrous version of this meeting, the difference comes down to three moves.

Bring the diff, not the doctrine. The two-crawl comparison and the view-source evidence make the problem concrete and sized. Engineers respect a reproducible bug infinitely more than a best-practice citation.

Scope it to routes, not the stack. Asking a team to abandon their framework is a war. Asking for server-delivered HTML on the twenty routes that earn revenue is a ticket. Modern frameworks make per-route rendering decisions genuinely cheap, and framing it that way turns the argument into a prioritization exercise.

Attach the money. Rendered-only pages that should rank represent a countable set of queries and a modelable amount of traffic and pipeline. I have watched a fix languish for two quarters as an SEO recommendation and ship in two weeks once it became "the category pages that should be earning an estimated X in monthly pipeline are invisible." Same fix. Different sentence.

A short war story, because theory never convinces anyone

A composite from real audit work, details blurred to protect the guilty. A B2B company relaunched on a modern JavaScript framework. The new site was faster in demos, prettier in every review, and organic traffic slid quarter after quarter while everyone blamed the content team. The content team, in turn, produced more content, which also did not rank, which produced more blame. By the time the site reached an audit, the working theory was that the entire industry had simply become too competitive.

The view-source test took under a minute: money pages shipped as a shell, everything meaningful rendered client-side. The two-crawl diff itemized it: a large share of the site's titles, descriptions and internal links existed only after rendering, and log data showed the rendering-dependent sections getting a fraction of the crawl attention. The fix was unglamorous: server rendering on public routes, real anchor navigation, meta tags in the initial HTML. No content was rewritten. Recovery started within weeks of the deploy, and the content published during the invisible year began ranking as if it were new, because to the machines reading the site, it was.

The lesson I took from that engagement has shaped how I work since: when good content fails at scale, suspect the plumbing before the prose. Writers are usually the first people blamed and the last people equipped to see the actual leak, which is exactly why a content strategist who can run these diagnostics is worth having in the room.

Framework-flavored footnotes

Without turning this into documentation, a few notes on how the common stacks behave in the wild:

  • React-based sites are where most problems live, simply because most sites are React-based. Pure client-rendered builds are the risk; the popular meta-frameworks on top of React make server rendering and static generation genuinely straightforward, so the fix usually already exists inside the tools the team is using.
  • Vue and its ecosystem follow the same pattern: the framework is fine, the rendering configuration is the decision. The equivalent meta-framework handles hybrid rendering per route.
  • Headless CMS setups deserve a special mention: the CMS is invisible to SEO outcomes, the front end is everything. I have seen teams switch CMS platforms to fix a problem that lived entirely in the rendering layer of their front end, an expensive way to change nothing.
  • Ecommerce platforms with app-like storefronts vary wildly. Before adopting any headless storefront, run the view-source test on a reference store built with it. Five minutes of due diligence beats a six-month migration regret.

The unifying point: in 2026, no mainstream framework forces you into bad SEO. Rendering strategy is a choice, made route by route, and the sites that suffer are the ones where nobody realized a choice was being made.

Frequently asked questions

Can Google index JavaScript sites or not?

It can, and it does, with delay, cost and per-page unreliability that scale with your site's size and your dependence on client rendering. "Can" was never the right question. The right question is whether you want your most valuable pages subject to a rendering queue and your AI-crawler visibility subject to a coin flip, when server-delivered HTML removes both risks.

Do I need to move my whole site to server rendering?

Almost certainly not. Apply the rule of thumb: URLs meant to earn traffic get server-delivered or pre-built content; app screens behind logins stay client-side. Hybrid, route-by-route rendering is standard in every major framework now, and the whole-site framing is usually what stalls the fix.

Does JavaScript SEO matter for AI search and ChatGPT visibility?

More than it matters for Google, honestly. Several AI crawlers execute little or no JavaScript, so client-rendered content simply does not exist for them. If getting cited in AI answers is part of your strategy, raw-HTML content on key pages is the entry requirement.

What is the fastest first step for a team with limited engineering time?

Run the view-source test on your top ten money pages today. If content and meta tags are present in the raw HTML, your problem is probably elsewhere. If they are not, you have just produced the one-line evidence that gets a rendering fix onto a sprint, and that single fix routinely outperforms months of content effort on a site that machines could not fully read.
