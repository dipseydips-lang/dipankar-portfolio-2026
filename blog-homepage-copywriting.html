Homepage Copywriting: The 7-Block Formula, Because Your Visitor Gives You Five Seconds

by Dipankar Mukherji·copywriting·~7 min read·target keyword: homepage copywriting

Your homepage has the hardest job in marketing: a stranger arrives with no context, decides in roughly five seconds whether this is for them, and either scrolls or leaves forever. Most homepages fail that window with poetry ("Empowering possibilities"), insider jargon, or a slideshow of everything the company has ever done. Having written homepages for manufacturers, engineering firms, agencies and gifting companies, here is the formula I use: seven blocks, in order, each with one job.

Before writing: pass the grunt test

The standard every homepage must clear: within five seconds, a distracted visitor should be able to grunt out three answers. What do you offer? How does it make my life better? What do I do next? Every clever headline that fails this test is a beautiful door to an empty room. Clarity converts; cleverness decorates.

Block 1: The header. Name the value, not the company mood.

One headline that says what you do and for whom, in words a customer would use. One subheadline that adds the differentiating detail. One primary button. Patterns that reliably work: the outcome ("Windows repaired at your door, at up to 70 percent less than replacement"), the audience call-out ("Field service software for contractors of 5 to 50 techs"), and the enemy ("Your techs are excellent. Your scheduling whiteboard is not."). The pattern that reliably fails: abstractions that could sit atop any company in any industry.

Block 2: Proof, immediately.

The claim in block one triggers an instant, silent "says who?" Answer it before the first scroll ends: client logos, a hard number ("2,500+ projects delivered"), a rating, or one devastating testimonial line. Proof placed early makes everything after it read differently.

Block 3: The problem, named.

Two or three sentences that describe the visitor's current pain so precisely they feel diagnosed. This is the most skipped block and the most bonding one: people buy from those who understand the problem, and understanding is demonstrated, not claimed. Write it from real customer language, not the boardroom's version of it.

Block 4: The offer, in three.

What you actually do, structured as three digestible items: services, steps or pillars. Three, because visitors are skimming and the brain holds three. Each gets a plain-language name, one benefit sentence and a link deeper. The homepage sells the click to the service page; the service page sells the inquiry. Asking the homepage to do both is how it ends up doing neither.

Block 5: The differentiator.

One section answering the question every serious buyer is silently asking: why you and not the tab next door? The honest answers are specific: the 27 years, the niche specialization, the process nobody else follows, the guarantee nobody else offers. If this section could be pasted onto a competitor's site without edits, it is not a differentiator. It is wallpaper.

Block 6: Proof, deeper.

Now the full-strength evidence: a case study strip with numbers ("193% organic traffic growth"), two or three testimonials with names and contexts, before-and-after specifics. Numbers beat adjectives; named humans beat anonymous praise; one detailed story beats six vague endorsements.

Block 7: The close.

A final call to action that lowers the threshold: say what happens next ("Tell me what you sell. I will tell you what to publish."), remove the risk (free audit, no-obligation quote, response within 24 hours), and repeat the primary button. Then stop. A homepage that ends decisively converts better than one that trails off into a footer of afterthoughts.

The mistakes that undo everything

  • We-we writing: count the "we" versus "you" on any draft. If we wins, rewrite. Visitors are the protagonist; you are the guide.
  • The everything homepage: serving four audiences with one page serves none. Pick the primary visitor; route the others with clear pathways.
  • Feature-speak: "AI-powered omnichannel platform" is a spec. "Answer every customer in one inbox" is a reason. Translate relentlessly.
  • Burying the button: the primary action should appear in the header, mid-page and at the close. People decide at different depths.

Frequently asked questions

How long should homepage copy be?

Long enough to complete the seven blocks, short enough that every sentence fights for its place: typically 500 to 900 words of visible copy. Considered, expensive services run longer; simple offers run shorter. Length is a budget spent on persuasion, not a target.

Does homepage copy matter for SEO?

Yes, mostly for your brand and category head terms, and increasingly for how AI systems summarize who you are. The clarity that converts humans is the same clarity machines extract. Write for the visitor; mark it up for the machines.

Should the homepage have a video or animation?

Only after the copy passes the grunt test without it. Media that clarifies earns its bandwidth; media that decorates slows the page and hides the message. Copy first, always, because the video will be skipped by most visitors and the words will not.
