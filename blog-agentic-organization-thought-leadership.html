The Agentic Organization Is Not a Faster Company. It Is a Different Company.

A research-backed essay on AI agents, software, expertise, management, measurement and the redesign of knowledge work.

Portfolio classification: Personal thought leadership and research-backed industry analysis. This is my own analysis and is not presented as client work.

For the last two years, much of the conversation around workplace AI has been framed as a productivity story.

Write the draft faster. Summarize the meeting. Generate the code. Analyze the spreadsheet. Create the presentation. Search the company knowledge base. Automate the repetitive task.

That framing made sense when generative AI was primarily a tool a person opened, prompted and then closed. It becomes increasingly inadequate when software can take a goal, plan a sequence of actions, use tools, inspect the results, recover from failures and continue until it reaches a defined state.

That is the important transition.

An AI assistant changes the speed at which a person performs a task. An agent can change who performs the task, how the task is coordinated, what information needs to be exposed to the worker, how performance is measured and even why the organization needs the old workflow in the first place.

That makes agentic AI less interesting as a software category than as an organizational design problem.

The question I keep coming back to is not, "How much work can an agent automate?"

It is: What happens to an organization when the cost of executing a well-defined piece of knowledge work approaches the cost of specifying, supervising and verifying it?

That is a much more uncomfortable question because it reaches beyond individual productivity. It touches management, career ladders, expertise, software architecture, organizational memory, quality control and the economics of junior work.

And the evidence emerging in 2026 is already telling us something important: AI adoption is moving faster than organizational redesign.

The Productivity Story Is Starting to Look Too Small

There is no shortage of evidence that people are using AI at work.

Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index, based on a survey of 20,000 AI-using knowledge workers across 10 markets and analysis of Microsoft 365 activity, describes a growing shift toward agents and multi-step workflows. Microsoft reports that active agents in its Microsoft 365 ecosystem grew 15 times year over year, while only 26% of surveyed AI users said their leadership was clearly and consistently aligned on AI. citeturn0search1

That juxtaposition matters more to me than the adoption number.

The technology is moving into workflows faster than management systems are adapting to it.

McKinsey's October 2026 analysis makes a similar point from another direction. It reports that 80% of respondents in its 2026 State of AI survey say AI has improved individual productivity, while only 37% report positive EBIT impact, and only 6% qualify as AI high performers. Its conclusion is that agentic AI requires organizations to rethink their operating models rather than simply add AI tools to existing work. citeturn0search0

This gap explains why I am skeptical of many "AI productivity" conversations.

If one employee can now complete in two hours what previously took five, that is useful. But the organizational question begins after the two hours.

Does the employee use the remaining three hours for higher-value work?

Does the company reduce cycle time?

Does it increase output?

Does it serve more customers?

Does it remove a bottleneck?

Does it redesign the team around the new capability?

Or does the old process remain intact while everyone quietly does more work inside it?

That last outcome is more common than the headline productivity numbers suggest.

AI Can Compress a Task Without Compressing a Process

This distinction is central.

Imagine a marketing team that spends three days producing a campaign brief.

An AI system reduces the writing component from six hours to one hour. On paper, the team has become five hours more productive.

But the campaign still needs a meeting. The legal review still happens on Tuesday. The brand approval still takes two days. The analytics tagging still waits for another team. The CRM workflow still requires a ticket. The final asset still moves through the same publishing queue.

The task got faster.

The system did not.

This is why agentic AI may have a larger organizational effect than copilots. An agent is potentially able to move across the boundaries that made the old process slow.

It can gather the information, draft the asset, check a specification, create a ticket, update a system, wait for an approval, continue after the approval and report the outcome.

Once software can operate across those boundaries, the bottleneck moves.

The bottleneck may no longer be execution.

It may be authorization.

Or judgment.

Or access to reliable data.

Or the lack of a machine-readable business rule.

Or the absence of a trustworthy evaluation mechanism.

This is where agentic systems become an organizational mirror. They expose the parts of a process that were previously held together by human memory, informal communication and institutional habit.

The Hidden Operating System of a Company Is Human Judgment

Most organizations contain far more undocumented knowledge than their process diagrams admit.

A sales manager knows which leads are worth escalating.

A senior engineer knows which warning signs usually precede a production failure.

A content strategist knows which source deserves more weight when two studies disagree.

A finance analyst knows which number in a report is technically correct but commercially misleading.

A project manager knows which stakeholder needs to be called before an issue becomes a meeting.

These decisions are rarely stored as explicit rules.

They live in people.

That is one reason organizations have historically needed layers of experienced employees. Expertise is not merely the ability to perform a task. It is the ability to recognize exceptions, interpret ambiguity and know when the normal process should not be followed.

Agents challenge this arrangement because they can execute explicit procedures cheaply while increasingly becoming useful at interpreting semi-structured information.

But this does not mean expertise becomes irrelevant.

It means expertise changes location.

Instead of spending most of the day executing the process, the expert may increasingly spend time defining the process, setting boundaries, evaluating edge cases and deciding when the system should not be trusted.

That is a fundamentally different job.

The Most Valuable Human Skill May Become Knowing Where the Agent Should Stop

There is a strange assumption embedded in a lot of AI discourse: that the ideal agent is one that can finish everything without human intervention.

I do not think that is the right design target.

A reliable agent should know when it has reached the boundary of its authority.

Consider three outcomes:

OutcomeWhat the system doesWhat it signals
ProceedConfidence and evidence meet the defined thresholdRoutine execution
EscalateEvidence is incomplete, conflicting or outside policyHuman judgment required
StopAction is unsafe, unauthorized or unverifiableSystem boundary reached

That third state is underrated.

In production systems, the ability to refuse an action can be more valuable than the ability to complete one.

A customer-service agent that invents a refund policy is worse than an agent that escalates. A finance agent that guesses missing transaction information is worse than one that requests evidence. A coding agent that silently changes security controls is worse than one that stops and asks for review.

The mature question is therefore not "Can the agent do this?"

It is "Under what conditions is the agent allowed to do this without a human?"

That question belongs to organizational design because someone has to define authority.

Software Is Becoming the First Place This Organizational Shift Becomes Obvious

Software development is an unusually useful laboratory for agentic work because software already has explicit artifacts, version control, automated tests, issue trackers, deployment systems and measurable outputs.

Agents can inspect repositories, reason over code, edit files, run tests, read documentation, investigate failures and create changes. The workflow is unusually compatible with machine execution.

But the research also gives us a warning against simplistic productivity claims.

METR's randomized study of experienced open-source developers, conducted in 2025 with then-current AI tools, found that developers using the tools took 19% longer on the studied tasks even though the developers themselves expected AI to reduce completion time. METR later reported that a subsequent study was difficult to interpret because AI adoption had changed participant selection and because multi-agent use complicated time measurement. METR concluded that AI likely accelerated some developers by early 2026, but its available data was not reliable enough to quantify the effect. citeturn0search9turn0search6

This is exactly why "AI makes developers 30% faster" is such a weak way to think about the future.

Speed depends on the task, the codebase, the developer, the tools, the verification system and the amount of context the agent can access.

More importantly, speed on an individual task does not necessarily equal throughput for the software organization.

If agents generate code faster than teams can review it, the bottleneck moves to review.

If review becomes faster, the bottleneck may move to testing.

If testing becomes automated, the bottleneck may move to product decisions.

If product decisions accelerate, the bottleneck may become customer research.

The organization does not simply become faster.

The constraint moves.

The Constraint-Migration Principle

I think this deserves to be treated as a principle for agentic organizations:

When AI removes a bottleneck, the value of the next bottleneck rises.

This sounds obvious, but it has serious implications for how companies invest.

Suppose an agent reduces content production time by 80%.

The obvious investment is more content.

The better question may be whether content production was ever the limiting factor.

If distribution, differentiation, research quality, brand trust and conversion measurement were the real constraints, producing five times more content may simply multiply the amount of average content entering the system.

The same pattern applies to software, customer support, sales operations and analysis.

AI can increase the supply of work before the organization has increased its capacity to evaluate that work.

That is a dangerous asymmetry.

When Generation Gets Cheap, Judgment Gets Expensive

This is perhaps the most important economic shift.

For years, organizations paid heavily for production because production required skilled labor.

As AI lowers the marginal cost of producing drafts, code, analysis, images, research summaries and operational actions, the scarce resource moves toward selection.

What should be produced?

What should be trusted?

What should be rejected?

What should be tested?

What should be escalated?

What is missing?

What does the evidence actually support?

This changes the value of expertise.

The expert who can generate ten reports is less differentiated when software can generate one hundred.

The expert who can determine which report should exist, what evidence it needs and what decision it should inform becomes more valuable.

This is why I do not think AI makes expertise disappear.

It makes shallow expertise easier to imitate and deep judgment more visible.

The Junior Work Problem Is More Important Than the Job Loss Headline

The most complicated workforce issue may not be mass replacement.

It may be the disappearance of the work through which people historically became experts.

Junior analysts used to learn by preparing reports.

Junior developers learned by fixing small bugs.

Junior marketers learned by writing campaign variants.

Junior researchers learned by collecting and comparing sources.

Junior consultants learned by building slides and cleaning datasets.

Much of this work was low leverage.

It was also training.

If agents absorb the repetitive layer, organizations still need a way to develop people into senior contributors.

This creates a problem that productivity dashboards will not capture.

A company can reduce junior work and look more efficient in the short term while weakening its future expertise pipeline.

The apprenticeship system can break quietly.

That means organizations need to redesign learning, not simply eliminate entry-level tasks.

A junior developer might spend less time manually implementing routine features and more time reviewing agent-generated changes, writing tests, tracing system behavior and learning architecture.

A junior content professional might spend less time formatting generic articles and more time conducting original research, interviewing subject-matter experts, validating claims and studying search behavior.

The training work still exists.

It has to be deliberately designed.

The New Career Ladder May Be Built Around Scope, Not Output

Traditional career progression often works roughly like this:

Do more work. Do harder work. Own larger projects. Manage people.

Agentic organizations complicate that model because raw output becomes less useful as a measure of seniority.

If an agent can produce 100 drafts, writing volume tells me very little about a person's value.

I would rather know:

  • How well does this person define problems?
  • Can they identify weak assumptions?
  • Can they design an evaluation system?
  • Can they recognize when an agent is wrong?
  • Can they build repeatable workflows?
  • Can they manage risk?
  • Can they make decisions with incomplete information?
  • Can they improve the system after observing failures?

This points toward a different career ladder: from task execution toward scope ownership.

The progression becomes less about producing more artifacts and more about taking responsibility for increasingly complex systems of work.

Management Changes When Employees Have Agents

There is another consequence that receives less attention.

Managers traditionally coordinate human capacity.

They allocate work, check progress, unblock people, review outputs and decide priorities.

When every employee can operate a small collection of agents, managers are no longer coordinating only people.

They are coordinating people plus machine capacity.

That changes the unit of planning.

A manager might ask:

"Who has capacity this week?"

The more interesting question becomes:

"What work should be executed by humans, what work should be executed by agents, and where should the two interact?"

That is closer to systems engineering than traditional task management.

Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index explicitly frames leadership as a need to redesign work and reports that organizational factors such as culture, manager support and talent practices account for more than twice the reported AI impact of individual factors in its analysis. The study is not proof of causation, but it strongly reinforces the idea that organizational context determines how much value individuals can extract from AI. citeturn0search1

Meetings Are One of the First Things That Should Become Embarrassing

This may sound like a small point, but I think it reveals the scale of the shift.

Many meetings exist because humans have to coordinate information.

Someone explains the current state. Someone asks what changed. Someone identifies a blocker. Someone assigns actions. Someone writes the notes. Someone follows up three days later.

Agents can increasingly perform much of the information movement around this process.

But that does not mean every meeting disappears.

The more useful distinction is between information synchronization and decision-making.

Information synchronization is increasingly automatable.

Decision-making is not.

If eight people are meeting for an hour because none of them knows the latest status, that meeting is an architectural failure in the information system.

If eight people are meeting because a consequential decision requires disagreement, judgment and accountability, that meeting may be exactly right.

Agentic organizations should become much more suspicious of meetings whose primary purpose is to move information between people.

The Agent Needs a Business Context, Not Just a Prompt

There is a common mistake in enterprise AI projects: treating the prompt as the interface.

For simple tasks, that can work.

For sustained agentic work, it is not enough.

An agent needs access to the right context:

  • Current business rules
  • Authoritative data sources
  • Permission boundaries
  • Definitions and taxonomies
  • Previous decisions
  • Relevant customer or product context
  • Tools it is allowed to use
  • Evaluation criteria
  • Escalation rules

This is why enterprise knowledge architecture suddenly matters much more.

The organization needs to know what is true, where that truth lives, who owns it and whether a machine can safely retrieve it.

In other words, agentic AI turns messy organizational information architecture into an operational constraint.

Bad Data Becomes an Active Failure Mode

Traditional software often fails loudly when data is missing or invalid.

Generative systems can fail more subtly.

An agent can receive incomplete information and still produce a coherent answer.

That is dangerous.

Coherence is not evidence.

An organization using agents therefore needs stronger distinctions between:

  • Known facts
  • Retrieved information
  • Derived conclusions
  • Model assumptions
  • Unverified claims
  • Human decisions

As agents become more autonomous, provenance becomes part of the workflow rather than a documentation afterthought.

If an agent recommends changing a contract, approving a transaction or escalating a customer, the system should ideally make it possible to reconstruct why.

That is not because every decision requires a philosophical audit trail.

It is because autonomous execution increases the cost of unexplained failure.

The Organization Needs an Evaluation Layer

This is where agentic AI starts to resemble production engineering more than chatbot experimentation.

A normal software function can often be tested against expected outputs.

An agent operates across longer trajectories. It may use several tools, make intermediate decisions, encounter failures and recover.

The final answer can look correct even if the process was dangerous.

For example, an agent could reach the correct customer record by querying an unauthorized system. It could produce the correct financial total after using stale data. It could generate the correct deployment while making unnecessary changes elsewhere in the repository.

So organizations need to evaluate more than the final answer.

They need to evaluate the path.

That means measuring things such as:

DimensionQuestion
OutcomeDid the task actually achieve its intended result?
Tool useDid the agent use the right tools and parameters?
EvidenceWere important decisions grounded in authoritative information?
PolicyDid the agent stay within its authority?
RecoveryDid it handle failure without creating additional risk?
EfficiencyDid it consume reasonable time, tokens, API calls and compute?
EscalationDid it involve a human when uncertainty exceeded its boundary?

This is one of the reasons I believe evaluation infrastructure will become a major enterprise software category.

The agent is not the complete product.

The agent plus the tools, permissions, data, evaluation, observability and recovery mechanisms is the product.

Multi-Agent Systems Will Not Automatically Be Better

There is another seductive idea emerging around agentic systems: if one agent is useful, several agents working together must be better.

Not necessarily.

Multiple agents introduce coordination overhead, duplicated work, inconsistent assumptions, communication costs and new failure modes.

Microsoft Research's CORPGEN work is interesting here because it specifically examines multi-horizon workplace tasks rather than isolated benchmark problems. Microsoft reported that leading computer-using agents in its multi-task environment saw completion rates fall sharply under larger interdependent workloads, illustrating how difficult long-horizon coordination remains. citeturn0search7

The lesson is not that multi-agent systems are bad.

The lesson is that decomposition is not free.

Breaking a problem into five agent tasks creates five opportunities for parallelism, but it also creates five interfaces where information can be lost.

The best architecture may therefore be a single capable agent with strong tools and evaluation rather than a collection of agents pretending to be an organization chart.

Agentic AI Could Make Organizations Smaller, But Not Necessarily Simpler

This is where the economics become interesting.

If a small team can operate agents that perform work previously distributed across multiple functions, some organizations may become smaller.

But smaller does not mean simpler.

A ten-person company using twenty specialized agents could have a more complicated operating system than a fifty-person company that relies on human processes.

Someone still has to own the workflows.

Someone has to monitor failures.

Someone has to manage permissions.

Someone has to update business rules.

Someone has to investigate anomalous behavior.

Someone has to maintain integrations.

Someone has to decide when a workflow should be retired.

The organization may shrink in headcount while growing in system complexity.

That means the future enterprise may have fewer people but more operational software.

The New Scarcity Is Not Intelligence. It Is Organizational Coherence.

AI models are becoming increasingly capable across reasoning, coding, multimodal understanding and tool use.

But capability alone does not create organizational value.

A company can have excellent models and terrible information architecture.

It can have sophisticated agents and unclear ownership.

It can automate workflows and measure the wrong outcomes.

It can generate more content and become less differentiated.

It can deploy coding agents and increase review debt.

It can automate customer support and reduce customer trust.

The missing ingredient is coherence.

Strategy, process, data, authority, technology, incentives and measurement need to point in the same direction.

That is a much harder problem than model selection.

Why AI Adoption Often Produces More Work Before It Produces Less

There is a paradox here.

Early AI adoption can increase work.

People experiment with prompts. Teams create internal guidelines. Managers review AI outputs. Developers fix generated code. Security teams approve tools. Legal teams establish policies. Marketing teams test new workflows. Employees create personal automations.

The organization may look less efficient before it becomes more efficient.

That is not necessarily failure.

It can be the cost of learning.

The danger is staying permanently in experimentation mode.

An organization should eventually convert experiments into systems.

That means documenting successful workflows, defining ownership, measuring outcomes, retiring ineffective ones and creating reusable components.

Microsoft describes this as moving from AI adoption toward AI absorption, where the organization captures what it learns from AI-assisted work and builds those lessons into shared routines. citeturn0search1

I think that distinction is useful.

Adoption asks: Are people using AI?

Absorption asks: Has the organization changed because AI is available?

The second question is much harder.

The Content Industry Is a Perfect Example

I work in content, so this is where the change becomes particularly obvious.

AI can already generate competent articles at extraordinary speed.

That makes the old content production model less defensible.

If a company competes by publishing generic explanations of topics that thousands of other sites can generate, production efficiency does not solve the strategic problem.

It makes the commodity cheaper.

Google's 2026 guidance for generative AI features explicitly emphasizes unique, non-commodity content. Google has also introduced features in AI Search designed to surface original content and firsthand perspectives, including Preferred Sources and a Highly Cited indicator. citeturn0search3turn0search8

This is not simply an SEO lesson.

It is a business lesson.

When generation becomes cheap, experience becomes more valuable.

Original reporting becomes more valuable.

Firsthand data becomes more valuable.

Expert interviews become more valuable.

Proprietary research becomes more valuable.

Strong editorial judgment becomes more valuable.

Clear opinions supported by evidence become more valuable.

In other words, the content professional's job moves upward.

The valuable skill is no longer "Can you write 2,000 words?"

It becomes "Can you find something worth saying that cannot be reconstructed from the same ten sources everyone else used?"

That Changes What a Portfolio Should Prove

This is also why I would not want my own portfolio to become a gallery of interchangeable AI-looking articles.

If AI makes generic production abundant, a portfolio that only proves production ability becomes weaker evidence.

I want a portfolio to show range of judgment.

Can I write technical content without flattening the subject?

Can I understand a cybersecurity product well enough to explain its architecture?

Can I write for an industrial company without turning engineering into marketing fluff?

Can I analyze Google Search changes without repeating the same "SEO is evolving" paragraph everyone has written?

Can I distinguish a client voice from my own voice?

Can I research a subject deeply enough to form an original position?

Can I build an article around an argument rather than a keyword?

Those are the capabilities that become more important as generation gets easier.

The Agentic Organization Needs Fewer Writers of Instructions and More Designers of Systems

Prompt engineering had a moment because prompts were the easiest visible interface to generative AI.

But the enterprise problem is moving beyond prompts.

A robust workflow requires more than instructions.

It requires inputs, tools, permissions, state, evaluation, exception handling, logging and human ownership.

The person designing that workflow is doing something closer to systems design.

They are asking:

  • What starts the workflow?
  • What information is authoritative?
  • What actions are permitted?
  • What must be verified?
  • What counts as success?
  • What happens when the agent is uncertain?
  • What gets recorded?
  • Who owns the failure?
  • How does the workflow improve?

That is much closer to operational engineering than prompt writing.

There Is a Political Problem Too

Organizations are not neutral machines.

People have incentives, status, expertise and power attached to existing processes.

When agents change who has access to information or who can execute work, they also change organizational power.

A junior employee with a powerful agent can potentially perform analysis that previously required several layers of support.

A small team can operate systems that previously required a large operations function.

A subject-matter expert can encode parts of their workflow into reusable systems.

That can democratize capability.

It can also create resistance.

Some employees will see agents as productivity tools.

Others will see them as threats.

Some managers will encourage experimentation.

Others will quietly penalize it because existing metrics make AI-assisted work look unusual.

Microsoft's 2026 research captures part of this tension: 65% of surveyed AI users reported fear of falling behind if they do not adapt quickly, while only 13% said they are rewarded for reinventing work even when the results do not meet expectations. citeturn0search1

That is a powerful organizational contradiction.

Companies can tell people to innovate while maintaining incentives that reward the old workflow.

The Transformation Paradox

This produces what I would call the transformation paradox:

The people most capable of changing the workflow may have the least incentive to disrupt the metrics used to judge their current performance.

If a content team is measured on article count, producing a better research system may look like underperformance.

If a developer is measured on tickets closed, spending time building an agentic testing workflow may reduce their ticket count.

If a customer-support team is measured on tickets resolved, designing an automated system that prevents tickets from being created may make the team look less productive.

AI transformation therefore requires changes to measurement.

You cannot redesign work while measuring the old work.

What Should Organizations Measure Instead?

The answer will differ by function, but the direction is consistent.

Old-style metricMore useful system-level question
Articles publishedQualified demand, useful reach, conversion and original information created
Tickets closedResolution quality, prevention, customer outcome and recurrence
Lines of codeReliable product outcomes, change quality and cycle time
Calls handledCustomer resolution and retention outcomes
Hours workedValue delivered and critical work completed
Reports producedDecision quality and time-to-decision

This is uncomfortable because output counts are easy.

Outcomes are harder.

But agentic systems make the difference impossible to ignore.

When software can produce the output, the output itself becomes a weaker measure of human contribution.

The Best Organizations Will Treat Agents as Infrastructure

I suspect the most successful companies will eventually stop talking about AI agents as a special category of software.

They will become part of the operating infrastructure.

Just as databases, cloud services, analytics and APIs are no longer treated as exotic technologies, agents will become components inside normal workflows.

A marketing system will contain agents.

A software delivery system will contain agents.

A finance system will contain agents.

A support system will contain agents.

The strategic question will shift from "Where can we use AI?" to "Which parts of our operating model should be executed by software, which should remain human, and where should they interact?"

That is a much more mature question.

What I Would Build If I Were Designing an Agentic Organization From Scratch

I would start with workflows, not models.

First, I would map the organization's recurring work and identify where time is lost through information transfer, repetitive execution, waiting, reconciliation and manual checking.

Second, I would separate work into three categories:

  • Automate: predictable work with clear success criteria and manageable risk.
  • Augment: work where human judgment remains central but AI can accelerate research, analysis or execution.
  • Protect: work where uncertainty, accountability or risk makes autonomous execution inappropriate.

Third, I would define authority before autonomy.

For every agentic workflow, I would specify what the system may read, what it may write, what it may change, what it may purchase, what it may communicate externally and when it must escalate.

Fourth, I would build evaluation before scaling.

If there is no reliable way to tell whether the agent is succeeding, scaling the workflow only scales uncertainty.

Fifth, I would capture the learning.

Every meaningful failure should become information that can improve the workflow, the evaluation rubric, the underlying data or the human process.

Finally, I would redesign the human roles around judgment, exception handling, system ownership and learning.

That last part is the one most organizations will be tempted to skip.

The Future Employee May Manage a Small Digital Workforce

This is where the language around "AI replacing jobs" becomes too blunt to be useful.

The more interesting scenario is that an employee increasingly becomes responsible for a portfolio of machine capabilities.

A content strategist may have research, analytics, competitive intelligence, drafting and QA agents.

A developer may have coding, testing, debugging and documentation agents.

A sales professional may have research, qualification, account intelligence and follow-up agents.

A finance professional may have reconciliation, anomaly detection and reporting agents.

The employee is not simply using AI.

They are operating a small digital workforce.

That changes the management ratio.

A manager who previously managed eight employees may eventually manage eight people who each operate multiple automated workflows.

The span of control could increase.

But so could the cost of mistakes.

The manager will need better observability, clearer policies and stronger evaluation.

The Organizational Unit May Become the Workflow

Traditional organizations are built around departments.

Marketing.

Sales.

Engineering.

Finance.

Support.

But many customer outcomes cross all of them.

A new customer might trigger marketing qualification, sales outreach, legal review, contract creation, provisioning, onboarding, billing and support.

Historically, humans moved the work between departments.

Agents can increasingly move information and execute actions across those boundaries.

This makes the workflow itself a more useful unit of analysis.

Instead of asking which department owns a task, organizations can ask who owns the complete outcome.

That could reduce the importance of some departmental boundaries.

It could also expose organizational designs that were created for administrative convenience rather than customer value.

The Real Competitive Advantage May Be an Organization That Learns Faster

Models are becoming widely available.

Agents are becoming easier to deploy.

AI capabilities are diffusing quickly.

That makes the model itself a weaker long-term differentiator.

The harder-to-copy advantage may be the organization's learning loop.

How quickly can it identify a useful workflow?

How quickly can it test it?

How accurately can it evaluate the result?

How quickly can it turn the lesson into a reusable system?

How safely can it scale that system?

How quickly can it retire workflows that stop creating value?

That is an organizational capability.

And it compounds.

A company that learns how to improve its agentic workflows becomes better at improving future workflows.

That is much more interesting than simply having access to the newest model.

The Agentic Company Will Still Need Humans. It Will Need Better Humans.

I do not think the endpoint is a company with no people.

I think the endpoint is a company where fewer people spend their time doing work that software can perform and more people spend their time deciding what should be done.

That sounds optimistic, but it comes with a condition.

Humans cannot outsource the ability to think.

If people stop developing domain knowledge because agents always provide the answer, organizations can become dependent on systems nobody truly understands.

If managers stop exercising judgment because dashboards summarize everything, the organization can become optimized for what is measurable rather than what matters.

If writers stop researching because models can draft, the web becomes increasingly full of summaries without firsthand knowledge.

If developers stop understanding code because agents write it, debugging becomes a negotiation with software nobody can confidently inspect.

The human advantage therefore cannot be "being human" as a vague philosophical category.

It has to be maintained through deliberate practice.

Judgment needs practice.

Research needs practice.

Writing needs practice.

Technical reasoning needs practice.

Decision-making needs practice.

AI should remove unnecessary execution, not necessary learning.

The difference is not how many agents a company has.

It is whether the company has changed because agents exist.

My Prediction for the Next Phase

I think the next phase of enterprise AI will be less spectacular in demos and more consequential inside ordinary workflows.

We will see fewer announcements about agents that can do everything and more systems quietly handling narrow but valuable processes.

Procurement checks.

Security triage.

Customer onboarding.

Code maintenance.

Research preparation.

Financial reconciliation.

Content intelligence.

Sales operations.

Internal knowledge retrieval.

The winners will not necessarily be the companies with the most agents.

They will be the companies that identify where autonomy actually creates economic value, build reliable boundaries around it and learn faster than competitors from the resulting data.

And that means the most important AI question for a CEO may eventually sound surprisingly mundane:

Which parts of our company are still organized around the assumption that humans must manually move information and execute routine knowledge work?

That question can uncover more value than another model benchmark.

The Final Shift Is From Producing Work to Designing Work

For most of modern economic history, professional status has been tied closely to the ability to produce.

Write the report.

Build the software.

Analyze the data.

Prepare the presentation.

Manage the account.

Run the process.

As agents absorb more execution, the center of gravity moves.

The scarce skill becomes designing the system that produces the right result.

That requires understanding the problem before touching the tool.

It requires knowing which information matters.

It requires defining quality.

It requires knowing what failure looks like.

It requires knowing when not to automate.

It requires building workflows that improve rather than merely repeat.

And it requires taking responsibility for the outcome even when a machine performed much of the execution.

That is why I do not think the agentic future is primarily a story about replacing workers.

It is a story about replacing assumptions.

The assumption that every workflow needs the same number of people.

The assumption that departments must remain the primary unit of organization.

The assumption that output volume is a good proxy for value.

The assumption that expertise is mainly execution.

The assumption that automation ends when a task runs without a human click.

And perhaps most importantly, the assumption that a faster version of the existing organization is the best version of the future.

I do not think it is.

The real opportunity is to build organizations around outcomes, judgment, learning and systems that can execute at machine speed without surrendering human responsibility.

That is not a faster company.

It is a different company.
