The Commodity Content Audit: Why Deleting Half Your Blog Can Grow Your Traffic

by Dipankar Mukherji·content strategy·~7 min read·target keyword: content audit

I recently audited 44 blog posts on an agency website, one by one. The finding: the large majority were what I call commodity content: posts that were factually fine, professionally written, and completely interchangeable with a hundred other pages on the internet. They earned almost no traffic, no rankings, no leads. They were not neutral, either. They were actively costing the site.

This piece explains what commodity content is, why it hurts more than it helps in 2026, and the exact audit process I use to decide what to update, what to merge and what to delete without flinching.

What commodity content actually is

Commodity content is any page whose information a reader could get, unchanged, from dozens of other sources. The tells:

  • The title is a generic question with a generic answer: "What Is Social Media Marketing?"
  • Nothing in the post required your company to exist. No client data, no original observation, no opinion anyone could disagree with.
  • It could be published on a competitor's blog tomorrow and nobody would notice, including the competitor.
  • It was written for a keyword, not for a reader, usually a keyword the site never had a realistic chance of winning.

A decade ago this content sort of worked, because search rewarded coverage. Today it fails twice over. Google's systems evaluate sites partly on overall content quality, so a blog that is 70 percent filler drags down the 30 percent that deserves to rank. And AI Overviews and chat assistants summarize commodity information instantly, so the residual traffic those posts earned is evaporating. Interchangeable content now gets treated exactly as its name suggests: substituted without notice.

Why deletion feels wrong and works anyway

Every stakeholder resists pruning. "We paid for those posts." "More pages means more chances to rank." Both intuitions are wrong in the same way: they treat content as inventory, where more is more. Search engines treat content as reputation, where the average matters. Fifty weak pages do not add fifty small chances. They subtract credibility from the pages that had a real chance.

Pruning concentrates three things: crawl attention onto pages that deserve it, internal link equity onto pages that convert, and the site's topical signal onto subjects where you have genuine authority. Sites that prune ruthlessly and reinvest the effort in fewer, stronger pages routinely see the surviving content rise. The blog gets smaller. The traffic does not.

The audit process, step by step

Step 1: Inventory everything

Export every blog URL with its metrics: organic sessions over the last 12 months, ranking keywords and positions, backlinks, conversions or assisted conversions, and publish/update dates. This used to take days of spreadsheet archaeology. With an AI workflow connected to the CMS and analytics, it takes an afternoon, which is exactly the kind of clerical work machines should be doing.

Step 2: Score each post on two axes

Performance: does it earn meaningful traffic, rankings, links or conversions? Potential: does the topic serve a buyer we want, on a keyword we can realistically win, where we have something original to add? Two axes, four quadrants, four verdicts.

PerformancePotentialVerdict
HighHighProtect and refresh on schedule
LowHighRewrite properly: new angle, proprietary insight, real optimization
HighLowKeep but stop investing; harvest its internal links
LowLowMerge into a stronger page or delete

Step 3: Merge before you delete

Five thin posts circling one topic usually contain one good article's worth of material. Consolidate them into a single substantial page, redirect the old URLs to it, and you convert clutter into authority. Deletion is for posts with nothing worth salvaging: no traffic, no links, no salvageable substance.

Step 4: Redirect with respect

Every removed URL gets a 301 redirect to the most relevant surviving page, not blanket-redirected to the homepage. Any post with real backlinks earns extra care, because those links are assets someone else paid you in trust. Then update internal links so the site stops pointing at ghosts.

Step 5: Reinvest the calendar

The audit's real product is not the deletion list. It is the reallocated capacity. Every hour that would have produced next month's commodity post now goes into content that clears a simple bar: it contains at least one thing only we can say. A client result, a dataset, a process, a defensible opinion. That bar, applied consistently, is the entire difference between a blog that fills a sitemap and a blog that fills a pipeline.

How to never need this audit again

Commodity content is not a writing failure. It is a planning failure. It gets approved when calendars are built from keyword lists alone, with no requirement for original input. The fix is structural: no brief enters the writing queue without a proprietary ingredient attached, and the SEO research (keywords, competitors, intent) arrives before drafting begins, not after. Teams that enforce those two rules stop producing filler, because filler can no longer get commissioned.

Frequently asked questions

How many posts should I expect to cut?

On blogs older than three years that were run on volume targets, it is common for half or more of the posts to land in the merge-or-delete quadrant. That number sounds alarming exactly once, and then the traffic curve makes the argument for you.

Will deleting content hurt my rankings?

Deleting the right content, with proper redirects, is far more likely to help than hurt, because the surviving pages inherit focus and equity. Deleting carelessly, ignoring backlinks and redirects, can absolutely hurt. The verdicts are strategy. The redirects are surgery. Do both properly.

How often should a content audit run?

A full audit yearly, a light quarterly review of the bottom performers, and a standing rule that every new post must beat the current average of the blog it joins. Auditing is much cheaper as a habit than as a rescue.

Can AI do this audit for me?

AI can and should do the inventory, the metric pulls and the first-pass scoring, which is most of the labor. The verdicts need a human who understands the business, because "low traffic" sometimes describes your most important sales-enablement page. Machines gather. Humans decide. That division of labor is the theme of every workflow I build.
