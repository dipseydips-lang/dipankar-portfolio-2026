Ecommerce Personalisation After the AI Rush: What Customers Actually Notice

by Dipankar Mukherji·Ecommerce / Food·~18 min read·target keyword: ecommerce personalisation

Personalisation became a buzzword before it became a customer experience

Online stores have been told for years that they need personalisation. Then AI made the word sound even more impressive. Suddenly every product recommendation box became an "AI-driven experience". But customers do not care what the box is called. They care whether the products are relevant, whether the search works, whether the checkout feels easy and whether the site remembers something useful without becoming creepy. The boring version of personalisation is often the better version.

One thing I keep noticing in this kind of work is that the first version of the explanation is usually cleaner than the real system. Real systems have exceptions. They have ownership questions, old documents, weird users, legacy integrations and the one edge case that appeared twice last month and now everybody is afraid of. That mess is not a reason to stop writing. It is the reason the writing becomes useful.

There is also a communication problem hiding inside the technical one. Engineers tend to describe components. Buyers describe outcomes. Operators describe interruptions. Compliance teams describe evidence. Good technical writing has to hold those views together without turning into a brochure. That means defining the thing, explaining the mechanism, then being honest about where the mechanism breaks.

Search is the first personalisation layer

Before recommendations or dynamic landing pages, there is search intent. Someone typing a specific product term has already told the store what they want. Poor search can undo a sophisticated personalisation stack. Synonyms, spelling mistakes, product attributes, availability and category logic all matter. The customer is not asking the site to be clever. They are asking it to understand a sentence and return something useful.

I would also resist the urge to turn every conclusion into a rule. Technology is full of trade-offs. A choice that is sensible for one workload can be wrong for another. A workflow that needs strict control may not need the same architecture as a low-risk internal tool. Context is not a disclaimer. Context is the actual answer.

Recommendations need context

A product can be "related" without being relevant. A buyer who just purchased a cake topper may need matching accessories, a different size or replacement stock, not another random item from the same category. Recommendation logic becomes more useful when it understands stage, intent and product relationships. The best systems are not necessarily the ones with the most complex models. They are the ones with a clear idea of what the recommendation is supposed to do.

There is a temptation to sound certain when writing about a fast-moving category. I would rather mark the boundary between what the documentation says, what the evidence shows and what I think follows from it. That small distinction makes technical writing more credible because the reader can see which part is fact, which part is interpretation and which part still needs testing.

And then there is implementation. A lot of technology writing stops at the point where a reader would actually need to make a decision. I prefer going one step further. What has to change in the workflow. Who owns the result. What has to be monitored. What breaks first. What happens when the assumption is wrong. Those details are where a niche article earns its length.

First-party data is less glamorous but more dependable

Store owners can build a useful picture from behaviour they legitimately collect: categories viewed, products purchased, searches made, subscriptions, repeat visits and stated preferences. The value is in what the data lets the store understand, not in the volume of it. Data should have a purpose. A giant event stream that nobody can turn into a customer decision is not sophistication.

Personalisation can also make a store worse

Too much dynamic behaviour creates inconsistency. A returning buyer may see a completely different page every time. A product can move because an algorithm decided a user likes a category, even though the product is exactly what they came to find. There is a point where relevance becomes unpredictability. Merchandising teams need controls, not just algorithms.

AI changes content production, not the need for judgement

Generative tools can create product copy or variation quickly, but store quality still depends on facts, specifications, use cases and brand voice. Google's guidance on generative AI in Search is useful here because it warns against producing many pages without adding value. Ecommerce sites already have a duplication problem. Thousands of thin, interchangeable descriptions do not become useful because they were generated faster.

The PDP still has to close the sale

Product detail pages are where the promises of personalisation eventually meet a decision. Images, dimensions, material, compatibility, shipping, returns and clear use information matter. A recommendation widget cannot compensate for missing information. In specialised ecommerce, the most persuasive page is often the one that answers the exact practical question a buyer has before purchasing.

Content strategy has to follow the catalogue

I like ecommerce content when it comes from the products themselves. What makes one product different. What goes wrong if a buyer uses it incorrectly. Which terms confuse first-time buyers. What accessories are actually compatible. What seasonal questions appear repeatedly in support. Those details create topics that generic keyword research will not always find. They also make the content feel written by somebody who understands the category.

The measurement problem

Revenue is the final metric, but stores need the path. Search terms, product views, add-to-cart events, checkout starts, purchases and repeat behaviour tell different stories. If an ecommerce team says personalisation increased revenue, the comparison period, traffic mix and attribution method matter. The claim is strongest when the analytics setup makes the change visible and reproducible.

My takeaway

The useful version of ecommerce personalisation is not a layer of AI on top of the store. It is a system that helps a customer find something relevant, understand it, trust it and buy it with less friction. AI can improve that system, but it does not replace merchandising judgement, product information or measurement. Relevance is still the product. The technology is only the mechanism.
